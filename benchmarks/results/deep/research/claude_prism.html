A. Verdict

Answer: Meta-analyses agree that sugary drinks are associated with higher type 2 diabetes risk (RR 1.25 per serving [c6]; RR 1.20 [c14]), but only 29 typed papers cover both exposure and outcome [c14], and news attention grew faster (2.7-fold [c5]) than research titles (1.5-fold [c3]).

Decision: Fund causal-design and low- and middle-income-country cohort work, not more descriptive counts.

Confidence: medium - three consistent pooled estimates [c6][c14], but a thin intersection literature [c14], no health-factor tally [c6], zero patents [c6].

Would change it: a high-tier contradicting study, or a populated claim-direction tally.

Measured. Argument. The data support one argument: the exposure-outcome association is consistently positive in pooled observational estimates (1.20 to 1.25 per serving or contrast [r1][r3]), yet the literature that explicitly pairs the exposure and outcome is small (29 typed papers [c14]) and the attention it receives is increasingly policy-shaped. Attention, evidence and geography of the sugar-sweetened beverage and type 2 diabetes literature, 2017-2025

Abstract

Background: Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) are a recurrent target of obesity and type 2 diabetes policy, yet it is unclear whether the volume of attention tracks the strength of evidence or its geography. Objective: We measured attention, evidence, geography and a short projection for the claim that SSB consumption increases the risk of type 2 diabetes. Methods: We queried a research index by title phrase (2017-2025), a news index by mention (yearly 2017-2025; monthly 2024-01 to 2026-05), a the health literature index-based geography service, a patent assignee lane and a claim-level evidence reader, and compared a no-change with a linear-drift projection using a one-step expanding backtest. Results: Research records with an SSB phrase in the title rose from 226 in 2017 to 331 in 2025 [c3]; news articles rose from 120 to 322 [c5]; the annual correlation was weak (r = 0.33, n = 9, derived from [c3][c5]). Pooled estimates for incident type 2 diabetes were RR 1.25 per serving [r1] and RR 1.20 [r3]; only 29 typed papers cover both exposure and outcome in a broad phrasing [c14], and none contradicted the claim [c14]. First-author affiliations are led by China (1,980) and the United States (1,960) [c8], whereas news is placed most often in the United States (306) and Nigeria (124) [c10]; no patents matched [c6]. A no-change projection (mean absolute error 33.25 versus 40.07 for drift) holds the series at 331 per year through 2028, with a sensitivity band of 115 to 547 [c9]. Conclusion: Evidence for association is consistent but observational and thinly paired in the typed literature; attention is growing faster in news than in research and sits in different countries from the authors.

Introduction

Sugar-sweetened beverages occupy an unusual place in nutrition science: they are a single, identifiable exposure that policy can tax, label or restrict, and they are linked in the epidemiological literature to weight gain and to type 2 diabetes. A pooled analysis of prospective cohort studies reports that each additional serving of SSB was associated with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes (RR 1.25; 95% CI 1.17 to 1.35; moderate certainty) [r1]. A separate meta-analysis of longitudinal studies reports RR 1.20 (95% CI 1.13 to 1.28) for type 2 diabetes and RR 1.17 (95% CI 1.10 to 1.25) for obesity [r3]. A review focused on Asia reports a greater risk with high consumption both before and after adjustment for BMI [r2].

Association estimates are only one layer of what a research funder or a workshop on the science of science needs to know. A second layer is attention: how much is being published and reported, and whether the two move together. A third is geography: whether the places that generate the evidence are the places where the topic is being debated. A fourth is the pipeline to products, for which patents are a crude proxy. This paper measures each layer for one claim, using a single set of declared queries, and it keeps the layers separate rather than blending them into a composite.

We pose five questions. RQ1: how have research and news attention moved over a stated window, and do they move together? RQ2: what does the clinical and health literature say about the claim, and where is it contested? RQ3: where is the subject studied, published, reported and patented? RQ4: what is a three-year projection of a measured series? RQ5: what single argument do the sources support, and what does it change for the choice of what to study or fund? We state in advance that the work is a descriptive measurement exercise on indexed records. It does not pool effect sizes itself, and no new epidemiological analysis was performed; effect sizes are quoted from the cited meta-analyses.

Data and methods

All queries were run on 2026-10-23 through one research and news intelligence service, and each is declared in the manifest as c1 to c18. Nothing was run that is not listed there.

Research attention (RQ1). A title-matched count of the research index was run for 2017-2025 using four case-insensitive phrases ("sugar-sweetened beverages", "sugar-sweetened beverage", "sugary drinks", "sugar sweetened beverages") [c3]. A second run added an outcome condition on the title containing "diabetes" or "type 2 diabetes" [c18]. These count records, not deduplicated studies. They are matched on title only, so papers that discuss SSBs without the phrase in the title are missed. The tool returned a saved history identifier used for the projection [c3][c9]. A separate typed-research slice (2017-2025) carries orientation and contribution labels, and supplied the composition counts and the orientation-by-year shares [c6][c12].

News attention (RQ1, RQ5). The news index was queried for the term "sugar-sweetened beverages" with a mention match, monthly from 2024-01-01 [c4] and yearly from 2017-01-01 to 2025-12-31 [c5]. The service censors the series at 2026-06-01, and the newest month (2026-05) is treated as possibly incomplete and is not plotted [c4][c2]. A three-term comparison (sugar-sweetened beverages, obesity, type 2 diabetes) used yearly counts for 2023-2025 [c17]. News themes for 2024-01 onward [c15] and a priority-7 sample of recent coverage [c16] supplied the drill-down.

Evidence (RQ2). The claim "sugar-sweetened beverage consumption increases the risk of type 2 diabetes" was passed to an evidence reader that retrieves papers in several lanes (most-cited, recent, newest, reviews, and the health literature index trial and meta-analysis lanes), tiers them by study design, and labels each read document supports, contradicts, qualifies or unrelated against the claim with a verified verbatim quote [c6]. A counter-evidence call used the variant "sugary drinks raise diabetes risk" to test lane sensitivity [c14] and the original claim for the corpus mix [c7]. A statistics call extracted verbatim figures [c13]. A research brief supplied the stated key numbers and institutions [c11]. The tier weights are the service's own; I did not alter them.

Geography (RQ3). One call returned four lanes: first-author affiliation country (the health literature index), journal country (the health literature index), country the news article is about, and patent assignee country [c8]. News geography at country level was queried with a mention match from 2019-01-01 [c10]. The lanes were never summed. Comparators for India were the United States, the United Kingdom, China and Australia. The mismatch indicator is news articles divided by first-author papers, multiplied by 100, a derived quantity whose windows differ.

Projection (RQ4). A conditional count extrapolation on the saved title-count history [c3] compared no-change with linear drift using an expanding-window, one-step-ahead backtest on 2022-2025 [c9]. Sensitivity bands are the worst one-step backtest error times horizon, not probabilities [c9].

Derived quantities. Percent changes, compound growth for research (4.9% a year from 226 to 331 over eight years [c3]), the 2017-indexed series, the ratio indicator and the Pearson correlation (r = 0.33, n = 9) were computed by hand from the tagged outputs [c3][c5]. The compound growth for news (13.1%) is the service's own figure [c5]. Date gaps to 2026-10-23 were counted in days.

Not done. We did not read full texts, did not pool effect sizes, did not run any patent search beyond the title match and assignee lane, did not deduplicate records across indices, and did not run statistical tests beyond the correlation.

Results

Attention. Research records with an SSB phrase in the title numbered 226, 182, 298, 346, 302, 311, 310, 259 and 331 from 2017 to 2025 [c3], a 46% rise from first to last year. Records pairing an SSB phrase with "diabetes" in the title numbered 10, 4, 11, 12, 7, 7, 7, 17 and 27 [c18], totalling 102. News articles numbered 120, 120, 124, 90, 147, 171, 231, 196 and 322 over the same years [c5], a 2.7-fold rise with compound growth of 13.1% a year [c5]. Monthly news from January 2024 to April 2026 ranged from 4 to 49 articles, peaking in October 2025 at 49 [c4]. The late third of the monthly window averaged 1.59 times the early third [c4]. The annual correlation between research and news was r = 0.33 (derived from [c3][c5]).

Evidence. The typed research slice holds 283 papers: 234 empirical, 33 review, 13 position and 3 methodological [c6]. By orientation, 147 are descriptive, 55 problem-identifying, 51 promising, 18 cautionary, 5 critical, 4 breakthrough and 3 comparative [c6]. By side, 55 support, 73 qualify, 5 contradict and 150 are neutral [c6]. The broader phrase set (1,115 typed papers) shows a contested share of 33.1% early and 34.9% late [c12]. Pooled estimates are RR 1.25 per serving [r1], RR 1.20 [r3], and RR 1.51 and 1.38 in Asia [r2]. In the first read, 2 documents supported the claim, 0 contradicted and 5 were unrelated [c6]; in the second, 3 supported, 0 contradicted and 2 were ungrounded [c14]. In the narrower phrasing, 3 papers covered both exposure and outcome [c6]; in the broader phrasing, 29 did, out of 917 on the subject and 104,068 on the outcome [c14]. The claim-direction table returned 1 claim, which cannot be summarised [c14].

Journal country: England 3,716, United States 3,325, Switzerland 1,326, Netherlands 1,011 [c8]. Discussion

The pooled estimates are consistent in direction. The per-serving estimate of 1.25 [r1] and the 1.20 estimate across 1.5 million individuals [r3] overlap in their confidence intervals (1.17 to 1.35 and 1.13 to 1.28), and the Asia review's BMI-adjusted estimate of 1.38 [r2] is larger though wider. The attenuation from 1.51 to 1.38 on BMI adjustment [r2] fits a partly adiposity-mediated pathway, and the 1.17 obesity estimate in the same meta-analysis as the 1.20 diabetes estimate [r3] shows the exposure's link to weight is of similar size. These sources are observational syntheses; their authors rate certainty as moderate in the case of [r1]. Our counts neither strengthen nor weaken those estimates; they show how thin the explicitly paired literature is in the typed index (29 papers [c14]) and that the tier-weighted read found no contradicting document [c6][c14].

The weak annual coupling between research and news (r = 0.33, derived [c3][c5]) suggests that news volume is not simply a lagged echo of publication volume. The news themes (health, nutrition, research [c15]) and the tag "taxation" on 84 articles [c15] point towards a policy story. Recent coverage illustrates this: a German report states that a federal levy on sugar-sweetened beverages is planned from 2028 with about 450 million euros per year in proceeds [r9][c16], and a Nigerian business group opposes a proposed additional tax [r10][c16]. Nigeria is the second most frequent place for news (124 articles [c10]) but is absent from the authorship top 12 [c8]. This echoes a general pattern, that a topic reported in one set of countries is often studied in another, but our data cannot say why.

The retrieval noise in the evidence reader (5 of 7 documents unrelated in the first read [c6]) is itself informative. Off-topic hits such as a breast cancer meta-analysis [r7] and a malaria vector-control trial [r8] entered because they matched the exposure term; any workshop reviewer repeating our queries should expect to filter. Randomised trials retrieved concern reducing SSB purchase or intake rather than diabetes incidence: a trial of pictorial warnings reports that 25% of parents bought SSBs after the intervention versus 55% before [r5], and a school-based meta-analysis finds interventions were effective in decreasing SSB consumption [r4]; a youth trial found no between-group differences at three and six months [r6]. These are exposure-change studies, which is a different link in the chain than the association with diabetes, and an incident-diabetes randomised trial was not retrieved.

For funders, the data suggest that the marginal value lies less in further confirmation of association and more in intervention-to-outcome evidence and in cohorts from countries where policy is being debated. That conclusion rests on counts of indexed records and on a handful of read documents, and the Limitations list what could overturn it.

Limitations

Data end dates. The title and typed research series end in 2025, about 296 days before 2026-10-23 (derived from [c3]); 2025 may still be filling through ingest lag, so the 46% rise could change. The news series is censored at 2026-06-01 and the newest month (2026-05, 7 articles [c4]) is incomplete in ways we cannot size; ingestion in June 2026 was 19.4% of baseline volume and later months were lower [c2]. Patents end in 2024 [c6] and publish roughly 18 months late, so the zero patent count may reflect lag, not absence.

Lane and matching. Research counts are title phrase matches, not abstract matches, and the four SSB phrases will miss "soft drink", "soda" and "sweetened drink" phrasings. The title-matched series (2,565 records [c3]) and the typed slice (283 [c6]) are different corpora and cannot be reconciled. News counts use a mention match: 127 articles in the evidence lane [c6] versus 1,521 in the yearly series [c5]. Obesity is not separately measured in the research series; only SSB titles and the SSB-plus-diabetes subset [c18] were counted.

Geography. First-author and journal countries are the health literature index-based and the service's counts cover the subject lane (11,867 records [c6]), whose window (1990-2025) differs from the news window (2019-2026). Only the top 12 (or top 15 for news) countries were returned, so grey countries on maps are unmeasured, not zero. Only 41.2% of matched articles were placed [c10], and the placed counts exceed the stated placed-article total (564 [c10]). Place resolution has known collisions. The ratio indicator mixes windows and is not a rate.

Evidence reading. Only 2 to 3 documents were read as supporting; the read sample is tiny and not a systematic review [c6][c14]. Quotes were verified against abstracts, but stance interpretation was not independently validated [c6]. The same Asian review is tiered as review and as meta-analysis in different lanes [c6][c14]. The claim-direction table returned one claim or none [c6][c14]. Orientation labels describe a paper's framing, not its finding [c6].

Projection. The model selection and evaluation share four folds with no independent holdout, bands are not confidence intervals, and structural breaks are not modelled [c9]. The 2025 rebound drives the band width.

What would change the conclusions. A high-tier contradicting study; a populated claim-direction tally pointing the other way; a later 2025 count revision; a patent search that uses CPC codes or product terms and finds assignees; or a placed-news set with fuller geography.
