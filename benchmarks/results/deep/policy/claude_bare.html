Sugar-sweetened beverages, obesity and type 2 diabetes: decision briefing

Prepared for the Group of Secretaries (Environment, Health, Finance, Science and Technology, PMO). Prepared 2026-10-23. Built from published sources recalled from memory; no data queries were run, so the calls list is empty. Every figure is tagged [rN] (reference) or (est.). Figures marked (est.) were recalled, not retrieved, and must be checked against the cited source before they are relied on.

Verdict

Answer: Cohort evidence consistently links higher sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) intake to higher type 2 diabetes risk: relative risk 1.26 for highest versus lowest intake [r1], and 1.13 per daily serving after adjusting for adiposity [r2]. India has a very large diabetes burden, 101 million adults [r4], but this briefing could not measure an India-specific SSB-diabetes effect or the effect of a tax.

Decision supported: Keep SSBs in the top consumption-tax tier (currently believed to be 40% (est.)), add front-of-pack warning labels, and fund India-specific measurement. Do not book diabetes reductions in the budget.

Confidence: Medium on direction of risk (consistent cohort meta-analyses [r1][r2]). Low on India-specific magnitude and on policy effect (no Indian effect estimate retrieved).

What would change it: Indian cohort estimates near 1.00 after adjustment, or evidence that taxes barely change purchases.

What the data shows

Measured (published estimates, not re-computed here):
  • Meta-analysis of prospective cohorts: highest versus lowest SSB intake, type 2 diabetes relative risk 1.26 (95% CI 1.12 to 1.41) [r1].
  • Meta-analysis of prospective cohorts: one serving per day, relative risk 1.18 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.32) before adjustment for adiposity, and 1.13 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.21) after [r2].
  • India, adults: 101 million with diabetes (prevalence 11.4%) and 136 million with prediabetes (prevalence 15.3%), from the ICMR-INDIAB survey [r4] (est. for exact digits).
  • Global modelled estimate: 184,000 deaths a year attributable to SSBs, of which 133,000 were diabetes, 45,000 cardiovascular and 6,000 cancer [r3] (est. for exact digits).
Interpretation (ours): The drop from 1.18 to 1.13 after adiposity adjustment [r2] suggests that part of the effect runs through weight gain, and that SSBs act on diabetes both through and beyond obesity. Cohort studies cannot exclude residual confounding. Nothing retrieved here links SSB intake to diabetes within India.
Limit: Newest sources: INDIAB (survey fieldwork to 2020, published 2023, gap to today about 3 years) [r4]; NCD-RisC (estimates for 2022, gap about 4 years) [r5]; IDF Atlas (2021, gap about 5 years) [r6]. Counts are published estimates, not our own queries.

Forecast: adults with diabetes in India

Adults (20 to 79) with diabetes, India, millions. Observed points: 72.9 (2017), 77.0 (2019), 74.2 (2021), all from [r6] (est.). Projection 2027 to 2029 is an estimate. Reading: the central path rises from 86.1 to 90.1 million (est.), with a wide band from 74.2 to 122.6 million (est.).
  • Method (est.): straight-line interpolation between the 2021 value (74.2 million [r6]) and the IDF projection for 2030 (92.1 million [r6] (est.)). Central 2027: 86.1, 2028: 88.1, 2029: 90.1 million (est.).
  • Bounds (est.): lower bound is no change from 2021 (74.2 million). Upper bound is the central path times 1.36, the ratio of INDIAB's 101 million [r4] to IDF's 74.2 million [r6] for the same year; this gives 117.1, 119.8 and 122.6 million. The bounds reflect disagreement between two sources, not a statistical interval.
  • Baseline and backtest: the no-change baseline was used as the lower bound. No backtest was run, so no error against a no-change baseline can be stated. The only in-sample check is a warning: IDF's own series fell from 77.0 to 74.2 million between 2019 and 2021 [r6] (est.), which reflects method revisions, not a real decline.
  • Last observed period: 2021, a gap of about 5 years to today (2026-10-23).
  • Measures: modelled counts of adults with diabetes (all causes). Does not measure: diabetes caused by SSBs, the effect of any policy, or type 1 diabetes separately.

Recommended option: Status quo tax tier plus Option A (labels) plus a funded measurement programme (see f1 to f6). It follows from the verdict: the risk direction is consistent [r1][r2] but India's effect size is unmeasured, so low-cost, reversible steps are justified and a new tax rate is not yet. It also follows from the forecast: even the lowest path stays at 74.2 million (est.), and the upper path reaches 122.6 million (est.), so the burden stays large either way and measurement is worth funding.

Strongest evidence against: the association weakens after adjusting for adiposity (1.18 to 1.13 per serving [r2]) and rests on observational cohorts, so SSBs may explain only a small part of India's diabetes burden. Forecast disagreement between sources (74.2 [r6] versus 101 million [r4]) also shows we cannot attribute any change to any policy.

Cost of being wrong: not measured. If we act and the effect is small, the cost is compliance burden and some revenue distortion. If we do not act and the effect is real, the burden continues. Neither side has a traced figure.

Method and limits

  • Newest data and gap to today: 2021 for IDF [r6] (about 5 years), 2022 for NCD-RisC [r5] (about 4 years), survey fieldwork to 2020 for INDIAB [r4] (about 6 years). Recent evidence after these dates is not included.
  • What counts are: published modelled estimates of adults with diabetes, not papers matching a phrase, articles about a place, or patents by assignee. Those three were not counted.
  • Lanes: where studied, where published, where reported and where patented are separate lanes and are never added together. All four are empty here.
  • Coverage and thin evidence: no Indian SSB-diabetes effect estimate; no Indian policy effect estimate. Closed by f5 and f6.
  • Censored periods: the latest year or month of any future literature or news series must be marked incomplete (f1, f2). Patents publish with a lag, so recent years undercount (f4).
  • Recall risk: figures marked (est.) were recalled and may differ in the last digit from the source. The team should verify each against its reference before the briefing.
