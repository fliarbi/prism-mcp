Do share buybacks reduce long-term corporate investment?

Verdict

  • Answer: The evidence retrieved here does not show that buybacks reduce corporate investment in general. None of the documents Prism read supports the claim, and one contradicts it [c1][c2]. From background knowledge, not this run, the best-known empirical result is narrower: firms that repurchase shares to just meet EPS (earnings per share) targets cut investment [r7].
  • Decision: Do not justify a broad buyback restriction or tax on the claim that buybacks reduce investment across firms. Focus scrutiny on specific red flags: buybacks timed to EPS targets, buybacks funded by debt, and buybacks that exceed earnings [r6][r7].
  • Confidence: low. Retrieval was thin: 9 of the 10 documents read were unrelated [c1]. The phrase "share repurchase" matched 0 titles [c1]. No effect sizes were extractable [c3].
  • What would change it: Quasi-experimental firm-level estimates showing investment falling after repurchases across broad samples.

Evidence

Contradicting (retrieved by Prism) - [r1] Herskovits, Muhle-Karbe & Tse, 2025, Mathematics and Financial Economics. Tier: peer-reviewed article. doi:10.1007/s11579-025-00396-0. - Claim it supports: in a model, buybacks and dividends leave investment unchanged. - Quote: "both dividend payouts and share buybacks result in the same shareholder welfare, distribution ratio and firm's investment level." [c1] - Caveat: this is a theoretical result. The paper also notes "examples where this equivalence breaks down due to distortions in managerial incentives or market frictions" [c3].

Context, not a verdict (retrieved by Prism, labelled unrelated to the claim) - [r2] DeAngelo, 2022, European Financial Management. Tier: peer-reviewed article. doi:10.1111/eufm.12407. - Quote: "Critics advocate increasing the tax on buybacks in the hope of inducing firms to invest more and in ways that benefit workers." [c1] - Use: describes the policy debate; it is not evidence of an effect. - [r3] Westphal & Zajac, 2001, Administrative Science Quarterly. Tier: peer-reviewed article. doi:10.2307/2667086. Cited by 785 [c1]. - Finding: many announced repurchase programs are "decoupled" and "remain more symbolic than substantive" [c1]. - Implication: announcements overstate the cash actually diverted. - [r4] Jamadar et al., 2024, Risks. Tier: peer-reviewed article. doi:10.3390/risks12100159. - Finding: UK firms repurchase "with surplus cash and Surplus investing cash flow" [c1]. - Implication: firms buy back with cash left over after investment, which is consistent with no crowding-out. - [r5] Firth & Yeung, 2005, Journal of Emerging Market Finance. Tier: peer-reviewed article. doi:10.1177/097265270500400301. - Quote: "firms initiating share repurchases have surplus cash and are undervalued." [c1] - [r6] The determinants of aggressive share buybacks… 2022, Tourism Economics. Tier: peer-reviewed article. - Finding: restaurant firms "spend amounts exceeding their earnings on share buybacks by depleting their cash reserves or even by borrowing money" [c3]. - Use: a risk signal; it does not measure investment.

Background (not retrieved or tier-scored by Prism; recalled from memory; no verbatim quotes verified) - [r7] Almeida, Fos & Kronlund, 2016, Journal of Financial Economics. - Qualifies the claim. Firms that would narrowly miss EPS forecasts are more likely to repurchase, and these EPS-driven repurchases are followed by lower investment and employment. - This is the strongest causal-design evidence for the claim, but it applies only to a subgroup. - [r8] Fried & Wang, 2018, Harvard Business Review. - Contradicts the aggregate claim. Net of equity issuance, payouts are smaller than gross buyback figures suggest, and capital is recycled to other firms. - [r9] Lazonick, 2014, Harvard Business Review. - Supports the claim. This is a position piece arguing that buybacks divert funds from productive investment. It is not causal evidence. - [r10] Gutiérrez & Philippon, 2017, Brookings Papers on Economic Activity. - Qualifies the claim. Weak US investment relative to Tobin's Q (market value relative to the replacement cost of assets) is linked to competition and governance factors, including the role of buybacks among quasi-indexer-owned firms.

Absent: no news items were read or quoted in this run. The 13 news articles [c1] are counted but not characterized.

Method and limits

Windows and gaps to today (2026-10-23) - Research ends 2025. That is a 296-day gap from 2025-12-31. - News ends 2026-06-01 (exclusive). That is a 144-day gap. - Readable papers span 1990–2025 [c1]. - Censoring: the news series is censored at 2026-06-01 [c3]. The research series reports censored_at: 2025, yet the 2025 point is not flagged partial [c3]. Treat the 2025 uptick with caution.

Lane and match mode - All counts are in the intersection lane [c1]. - Matching is by title phrase and curated topic. The phrases "share repurchase" and "stock repurchase" matched 0 titles [c1], which is implausible for the field. Counts are therefore a lower bound on relevant literature, not a census.

Coverage - All tables passed strict index partition reads. Prism notes this is "reachability and historical row floor, not an ingest completeness proof" [c1].

What the counts are and are not - The 27/3/4/6/14 figures are framing labels on each paper's own stance, not verdicts on the claim [c1]. - Only 10 documents were read, and 1 of them bore on the claim [c1]. The verdict therefore rests heavily on background references [r7]–[r10], which Prism did not retrieve, tier-score, or quote-verify.

What this run cannot answer - Effect sizes with a population: none extracted [c3]. - Aggregate macro investment trends. - R&D versus capex splits. - Non-US evidence beyond the UK and Hong Kong papers. - The content of the 13 news articles.

Interpretation - The verdict that the subgroup effect is real but the general effect is unproven is our synthesis, not a Prism output.
